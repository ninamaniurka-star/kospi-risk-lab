The Korean market story

From a 12-company exchange in 1956 to the most volatile major market of 2026. Click a chapter to open it.

ShowHide
1956–1989 · A market is born

The Korea Stock Exchange opened on 3 March 1956 with just 12 listed companies, at a time when trading was mostly in government bonds. The KOSPI as we know it arrived in 1983: a market-cap-weighted index of every common stock on the main board, set to 100 on 4 January 1980.

Through the 1980s the market was led by construction, finance and trading houses, the engines of the "Miracle on the Han River". The index first broke 1,000 on 31 March 1989, then spent more than a decade bouncing below and above that line.

1983 · Korea bets on memory chips

In February 1983, Samsung founder Lee Byung-chul announced from Tokyo that Samsung would enter semiconductors, the so-called Tokyo Declaration, against strong opposition at home. Samsung chose DRAM, a mass-producible memory chip, and had a working 64K DRAM by December 1983, making Korea the third country after the US and Japan to build one.

Within ten years the gamble paid off. In 1992 Samsung produced the world's first 64-megabit DRAM and became the world's largest DRAM maker, a position it has held since.

The same year, 1983, the Hyundai group founded Hyundai Electronics. After the 1997 crisis it became Hynix Semiconductor in 2001, went through years of creditor control, and in 2012 was bought by the SK Group as SK Hynix. Together, these two companies are the core of this portfolio.

1997 · The won breaks

In the mid-1990s Korea held a strong-won policy. Banks and the chaebol borrowed heavily in short-term dollars at low rates. When the Asian crisis spread from Thailand in 1997, foreign lenders pulled out and everyone needed dollars at once.

On 17 November 1997 the Bank of Korea stopped defending the currency, and the won fell below 1,000 per dollar for the first time. Four days later Korea asked the IMF for help, and on 3 December the IMF approved a $57 billion package, the largest it had ever arranged. By late December the won had lost about 55% of its value, from roughly 844 per dollar on average in 1996 to around 1,850 in 1997.

This is why the model treats the currency as a risk factor. When Korean stocks crash, foreign money leaves and the won falls with them, so a foreign investor is hit twice. That is the negative correlation between EUR/KRW and the stocks in the heatmap above.

2000s–2024 · The "Korea discount"

After the IT boom of 1999 and the 2008 crisis, the KOSPI settled into a long sideways range. Traders nicknamed it the "Boxpi", stuck roughly between 2,000 and 2,300 for years.

Korean companies traded at lower valuations than peers in other markets, many below the value of their own assets. The usual explanations were conglomerate (chaebol) structures that put family control ahead of minority shareholders, low dividends, and the risk from North Korea.

In 2024 the government launched the Corporate Value-Up Program, inspired by Japan's reforms. It asked listed companies to publish plans to raise their value and return more cash to shareholders.

2012–2024 · SK Hynix and the AI chip

When SK bought Hynix in 2012, many called the deal financially irresponsible. SK Hynix then bet on high-bandwidth memory (HBM), stacked memory chips that feed data very fast. In 2013 it worked with AMD to get the first HBM standard published. For years HBM was a niche product, and around 2019 its packaging plant stood half idle.

ChatGPT changed that in late 2022. Nvidia's AI accelerators need HBM, and SK Hynix became Nvidia's main supplier, with about 55% of the HBM market in 2024 according to JPMorgan, ahead of Samsung. This later made SK Hynix Korea's most valuable company. It also tied the whole Korean market to one question: does the AI spending boom continue?

2025 · The best market in the world

The KOSPI started 2025 near 2,400, weighed down by the political shock of a failed martial-law declaration. It ended the year up about 75%, the best-performing major index in the world and its strongest year since 1999.

Two forces drove it. The first was the AI boom in memory chips. The second was reform: President Lee Jae-myung campaigned on "KOSPI 5,000", and his government amended the Commercial Act to protect minority shareholders, with a third round passed in February 2026. Foreign investors poured in.

2026 · Up to 9,000, then the fall

The rally kept going. The KOSPI broke 5,000 in January 2026 and 6,000 in February, and by May it had roughly tripled from the end of 2024. It peaked above 9,000 on 22 June.

Then it turned. With Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix making up roughly half the index, every doubt about AI spending hit the whole market at once. Foreign investors became heavy sellers, circuit breakers were triggered 10 times in the year by mid-October, and in October the index moved 3.58% a day on average. On 5–6 November it fell about 11% in two days, closing at 5,059.69. One economist called it more volatile than Bitcoin.

2026 · The won's round trip

The won followed the same story with a lag. Heavy foreign selling of Korean stocks and the Iran war pushed it close to 1,600 per dollar by June 2026.

From July it recovered sharply. Strong exports, a large trade surplus and Bank of Korea rate hikes to 3% in July and August brought it back to about 1,340 per dollar in October, its strongest since September 2024.

For this portfolio the relevant rate is EUR/KRW, which combines the won's moves against the dollar with the euro's own moves against the dollar.

What this means for the model

Concentration: half the portfolio is in Samsung and SK Hynix, just like the market. The risk contribution chart shows that most of the tail loss comes from those two.

Currency: 1997 and 2026 both show the won falling when stocks fall. An unhedged euro investor takes that loss on top, which is what the "Currency adds" figure measures.

Fat tails: a 6% daily fall is close to impossible under a normal distribution with 2026-level volatility, yet it happened twice in a row. That is why the page offers Student-t and bootstrap scenarios, and stress tests for moves no model expects.

Volatility shifts: calm years followed by violent ones break the constant-volatility assumption. Use the volatility shock lever to see what a 2026-style regime does to VaR.

How the model works

Each step explained so you can talk through it in an interview. Click a step to open it.

ShowHide
1. Prices follow geometric Brownian motion

Each stock price S and the exchange rate follow a geometric Brownian motion (GBM): the price grows at a drift μ and is hit by random shocks proportional to its level, with volatility σ.

dSt = μ St dt + σ St dWt

Solving it (Itô's lemma) gives the price after a step Δt. Log returns are normal, so prices stay positive and are lognormal.

St+Δt = St · exp[(μ − ½σ²)Δt + σ√Δt · Z], Z ~ N(0,1)

The −½σ² term is the volatility drag. The model estimates the average daily log return, which already equals (μ − ½σ²)Δt, so no further correction is needed. With drift switched off that term is set to zero, the usual choice for horizons of a few weeks, because the mean is tiny and badly estimated.

2. Estimating from history

Daily log returns r = ln(Pt/Pt−1) give the mean, the volatility of each factor and the 6×6 covariance matrix Σ for the 5 stocks plus EUR/KRW. Annualised volatility = daily σ × √252.

3. Correlated shocks: Cholesky

The six factors move together, so their shocks can't be drawn independently. Σ is factorised as Σ = L·Lᵀ, where L is lower triangular. Multiplying a vector of independent normals by L gives shocks with exactly the right covariance.

x = m·h + √h · L·z, z ~ N(0, I6) ⇒ Cov(x) = h·Σ

The correlation stress lever blends the correlation matrix toward ±1, ρ′ = (1−λ)ρ + λ·sisj, with s = +1 for stocks and −1 for the currency. This is a mix of valid matrices, so it stays valid.

4. Converting to euros

EUR/KRW is quoted as won per euro, X. A Korean stock is worth P/X euros, so its euro log return is rstock − rX. When the won weakens, X rises and the investor loses. Each scenario revalues the whole portfolio:

P&L = V · [ Σi wi · exp(xi − (1−H)·xFX) − 1 ]

H is the hedge ratio (hedging cost and the interest-rate differential are ignored). Setting H = 100% isolates pure equity risk, which is how the page measures the currency effect.

5. Three ways to draw scenarios

GBM (normal): the textbook model above. Fast and transparent, but normal tails are too thin for real markets.

Student-t: each day's normal shock vector is divided by √(χ²ν/ν) and rescaled to unit variance. That keeps the volatility but adds fat tails and joint crashes, and lower ν means fatter tails. The 10-day return is the sum of daily draws.

Bootstrap (historical simulation): the model draws whole past days at random, with all six returns from the same day, and adds them up over the horizon. It makes no distribution assumption and keeps real fat tails and co-movement, but it can't produce a crash worse than the worst day in the sample.

6. Value-at-Risk and Expected Shortfall

Sort the simulated P&L. VaR at confidence c is the loss at the (1−c) percentile: "with 99% confidence we won't lose more than this over h days". Expected Shortfall is the average loss beyond that point. It answers "how bad is it when it is bad?" and is coherent (it rewards diversification). Basel's FRTB rules replaced 99% VaR with 97.5% ES for this reason.

VaRc = −q1−c(P&L) ESc = −E[P&L | P&L ≤ −VaRc]

Cross-checks: parametric VaR = zc·σp·√h·V with σp² = bᵀΣb, where b is the vector of weights with the currency at −(1−H). Historical VaR takes the 1-day percentile of actual portfolio returns and scales it by √h.

7. Risk contributions

In each tail scenario the loss is split into each stock's move in won plus a currency remainder. Averaging those parts over the tail gives each position's share of ES (Euler allocation). The shares add up exactly to ES.

8. Backtesting with the Kupiec test

For every day after the first 250, the page computes a 1-day parametric VaR from the previous 250 days and counts the days that lost more. If the model is right, breaches follow a Binomial(T, 1−c). Kupiec's likelihood ratio, compared with χ²(1), tests whether there are too many or too few:

LR = −2 ln[(1−p)T−x px] + 2 ln[(1−x/T)T−x (x/T)x]
Limitations

Volatility and correlation are constant, while real markets cluster (GARCH or EWMA would react). The √h scaling and summing days assume returns are independent from day to day. There is no rebalancing, dividends, liquidity or transaction costs. Simulation noise is real, as the convergence chart shows.
